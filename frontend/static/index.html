STEP 4

What each option means

Why these options? (and what we left out)
    Plain-English glossary
    • Expected return: the yearly gain the past data suggests. An estimate, not a promise.
    • Risk: how much the value usually swings in a year. Higher means a bumpier ride.
    • Cost to trade: brokerage and charges to move from today’s holdings to the option (estimated rates: 0.119% on buys, 0.104% on sells).
    • Pareto front: the portfolios where you cannot get more of one thing without giving up another.
    • Balanced: the option closest to the ideal on all three goals at once.

    How the method was tested

    The project’s own results on real NSE prices (30 independent runs of each method). The app uses Hybrid+archive.

    The honest part: out of sample

    We rebalanced every quarter for 15 quarters using only past data, then looked at what really happened.

    Take-away: the method finds better-spread trade-offs, but better-looking estimates did not turn into better real returns, because expected returns estimated from past prices are noisy. That is why the app asks you to weigh risk and cost rather than trust the return number.

    What this is

    PortfolioPilot is a student project (Optimization Techniques). It treats rebalancing as a three-goal problem (return, risk, trading cost) with real-world rules, and shows the best trade-offs.

    Limits you should know

    • Not investment advice. It is an educational tool.
    • Expected returns are plain averages of past prices; they are noisy and optimistic.
    • Costs are proportional estimates; they leave out tax, bid-ask spread and market impact.
    • 30 large NSE stocks only, long-only, at most 10 holdings.
    • The default “current portfolio” is a made-up example; edit it to match yours.
    • One click uses one frozen snapshot; it is not a live feed.

    How an answer is produced

    1. Freeze prices and estimate return and risk from the last ~3 years.
    2. Search with NSGA-II (an evolutionary algorithm) for which stocks to hold.
    3. Polish the weights exactly with a convex solver.
    4. Keep the non-dominated portfolios, choose a few well-spread ones, and explain them.
